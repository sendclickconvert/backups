A direct mail brochure is not a company overview. It's a sales tool with a job: move a qualified prospect from awareness to readiness to call. Every panel, every headline, every image should serve that movement.

At [Cornerstone Services](/services/graphic-design) in New Paltz, we design brochures for businesses across Ulster, Dutchess, and Orange counties — primarily for campaigns where the service is complex enough that a postcard can't carry the full sales argument. A law firm, a financial advisor, an HVAC company with multiple product lines, a dentist explaining cosmetic procedures: these businesses need a format that can carry more.

Here's how to structure a brochure that actually gets read.

## Start With the Job, Not the Format

Before choosing a fold or size, define what the brochure needs to accomplish:

**Immediate-response campaign piece:** The brochure arrives in the mail and the recipient should call or visit within 48 hours. Design priority: offer, urgency, and CTA. Include a coupon, deadline, or QR code that creates action.

**Sales support piece:** The brochure is handed out at an appointment, trade show, or event. The prospect is somewhat qualified. Design priority: credibility, service detail, and differentiation. Include credentials, process explanation, and testimonials.

**Leave-behind reference piece:** The prospect already talked to you and now has the brochure to review. Design priority: detailed information, FAQs answered, reassurance. Include pricing ranges, guarantees, and contact information prominently.

Each of these requires a different content emphasis. Designing one brochure to accomplish all three typically produces one that accomplishes none of them well.

## Panel-by-Panel Structure for a Tri-Fold

A standard 8.5x11 tri-fold has six panels. Here's how each should function:

### Front Cover (Right Outer Panel)

The cover is the most consequential design decision in the brochure. It determines whether the piece gets opened.

**What to put on the cover:**
- A compelling headline — the offer, primary benefit, or a question that creates curiosity
- One strong visual that supports the headline
- Your logo and business name (small — credentialing, not headlining)

**What NOT to put on the cover:**
- The company name as the headline
- A tagline as the sole text
- Multiple images or a collage
- "We've been serving the Hudson Valley since 1998" (true but not a reason to open)

The cover test: hold the brochure face-out and ask someone who hasn't seen it: "Would you open this?" If they hesitate, the cover needs revision.

### Back Panel (Center Outer Panel)

The back panel is seen before the brochure is opened — it faces up in a mail stack when the front faces down. Many readers scan the back before deciding whether to open the piece.

**Back panel content:**
- A concise summary of the primary offer or service
- A secondary CTA (phone number, URL) for readers who won't open
- Your physical address and contact information
- If applicable: a testimonial or social proof statement ("Trusted by over 500 Hudson Valley families")

### Left Outer Panel (Tucked Panel)

This panel is the last thing seen when the brochure is fully unfolded — the reader has already committed to opening it. Use it for:

- Contact information (comprehensive: phone, email, address, hours)
- Service area list: "Serving New Paltz, Kingston, Poughkeepsie, Woodstock, and the surrounding Hudson Valley"
- Secondary offers or package descriptions
- A step-by-step process ("How it works: 1. Call for a free estimate. 2. We assess your property. 3. We complete the work.")

### Inside Three Panels (The Full Story)

The inside spread is where you make the full case. Structure it:

**Panel 4 (left inside, first seen when opened):**  
The problem statement or need. Connect with where the reader is: "If you're tired of [problem], you're not alone." Or lead with your strongest differentiator.

**Panel 5 (center inside, the reader's eye naturally goes here):**  
The service or solution. Detail what you offer, how it works, and what results the reader can expect. Bullet points over paragraphs. Use a header for each service or feature.

**Panel 6 (right inside, natural reading conclusion):**  
Social proof + CTA. One or two testimonials, your credentials or certifications (Sean Griffin, MDP, Cornerstone Services, founded 1998), and a prominent call to action with a phone number in large type.

## Typography Rules for Brochures

**Headers:** 18–24 pt., bold. One per content section.

**Body copy:** 10–12 pt. for normal reading conditions. Do not go below 9 pt. — brochures are often read in lower light than monitors.

**CTA/phone number:** 16–20 pt. bold. Should be immediately locatable by a reader scanning for contact information.

**Testimonials:** Set in a different style from body copy — italic or a color box — to create visual separation. Include first name and town: "James R., Kingston, NY."

## USPS Compliance for Self-Mailing Brochures

If the brochure will be mailed without an envelope, it must comply with USPS self-mailer requirements:

- All open edges must be tabbed (wafer tabs on the open side)
- The address panel must be on the outer face in a clear zone
- The indicia area (upper right) must be kept clear of graphics and text
- The barcode clear zone (bottom 5/8 inch of the address face) must be completely blank

We design for USPS compliance at Cornerstone — self-mailing brochures are set up so the address side functions correctly after folding and tabbing.

## Paper Stock and Finish Selection for Brochures

Paper choice affects both the tactile quality and the cost of the brochure. For direct mail brochures:

**100 lb. gloss text:** The standard for most tri-fold brochures. Bright, sharp photo reproduction with a smooth finish that feels professional without being heavy. Folds cleanly. Cost-effective for quantities of 500–5,000. This is what we print most direct mail brochures on at Cornerstone.

**100 lb. matte text:** Same weight as gloss text but with a non-reflective finish. Better for text-heavy brochures where glare from overhead light interferes with reading. Preferred by professional services (law firms, financial advisors) where the matte finish communicates understated quality.

**80 lb. cover stock:** Heavier weight used for self-mailing brochures that need to survive postal processing without an envelope. Required for USPS self-mailer compliance. The additional weight adds cost but eliminates the need for an outer envelope, which can reduce total production cost for simpler campaigns.

**UV coating vs. aqueous coating:** UV coating adds a high-gloss protective layer that makes colors pop and protects against scuffing in the mail. Aqueous coating is a water-based protective layer that is less glossy but allows writing on the surface (important if the brochure includes a fill-in form or reply area). For direct mail, UV coating on the cover panels and aqueous on the inside is a common combination.

## Common Brochure Sizes Beyond the Tri-Fold

While the 8.5x11 tri-fold is the standard, other formats serve specific purposes:

**8.5x14 tri-fold (legal size):** 14% more content space per panel. Useful when the standard tri-fold is too cramped for the content. Slightly higher paper cost but the same folding and production process.

**11x17 bi-fold (half-fold):** Four large panels (8.5x11 each when folded). Ideal for businesses with extensive service descriptions, large product photography, or complex process explanations. The larger panel size allows more visual breathing room and larger typography.

**11x25.5 gate-fold:** A dramatic reveal format where two outer panels fold inward to meet in the center, then open to reveal a wide interior spread. Best for premium brands, luxury services, or any campaign where the unfolding experience itself creates engagement. More expensive to fold and finish, but the format itself communicates quality.

**Z-fold (accordion fold):** Three panels that fold in alternating directions, creating a zigzag when partially opened. Each panel can function as a standalone section. Effective for step-by-step presentations or before/after sequences where each panel reveals the next stage.

At Cornerstone, we produce all of these formats in-house. The choice between them depends on content volume, brand positioning, mailing method (self-mailer vs. envelope), and budget. We'll recommend the format that best fits your campaign goals during the quote process.

## Content Strategy: What to Include on Each Panel

The most common content mistake in brochure design is treating it like a website — trying to put everything the business does on six panels. A brochure with a clear content strategy outperforms one that tries to be comprehensive.

**For service businesses (HVAC, landscaping, legal, dental):**
- Cover: the primary service or seasonal offer
- Inside panels: 3–4 key services with brief descriptions (2–3 sentences each), one strong testimonial, credentials and certifications
- Back panel: phone number, address, service area, hours
- Tucked panel: step-by-step process ("How it works")

**For nonprofits (fundraising brochures):**
- Cover: the impact story headline ("Last year, 847 families walked through our doors")
- Inside panels: the mission in action (one compelling story, key statistics, donation impact breakdown), reply/giving information
- Back panel: contact information, website, QR code for online giving
- Tucked panel: board of directors, credentials, organizational history

**For real estate and property businesses:**
- Cover: the primary selling point (location, lifestyle, amenity)
- Inside panels: property features, floor plans or community maps, pricing or financing information
- Back panel: agent contact information, brokerage branding
- Tucked panel: neighborhood highlights, school ratings, commute times

Each of these structures follows the same principle: the cover creates interest, the inside delivers the full story, and the back panel enables action for readers who skip the interior entirely.
