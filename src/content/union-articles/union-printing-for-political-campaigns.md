Every election cycle, candidates learn the union bug lesson the hard way: they show up to a union endorsement hearing with a piece of campaign literature — well-designed, professionally printed, and missing the union bug. The labor committee chair asks about it. The candidate wasn't aware their printer wasn't union-affiliated. The conversation changes.

The union bug is not a large graphic element. It's a small, 1/4-inch imprint in the corner of the piece. But its presence or absence tells an organized labor audience something immediate about how seriously the candidate takes labor values in practice, not just in speeches.

At [Cornerstone Services](/services/union-printing) in New Paltz, we've produced union-label campaign materials for Hudson Valley political campaigns for over 28 years. Here's what campaigns need to get right.

## Who Checks for the Union Bug

**Union endorsement committees.** Labor organizations in the Hudson Valley — building trades councils, CSEA locals, healthcare worker unions, teachers' unions, Teamster locals — have endorsement processes that examine whether the candidate's campaign is practicing labor solidarity. A stack of non-union-printed literature at an endorsement presentation is a concrete example of not doing so.

**Union member campaign workers.** Campaign volunteers who are union members notice the bug (or its absence) on the materials they're asked to distribute. The absence creates a credibility problem that is harder to address in conversation than it is to prevent.

**Opposition research.** In competitive races, opposition campaigns have used the absence of the union bug as a contrast message with union-household voters. "Candidate X says they support workers, but their campaign material was printed non-union" is a specific, verifiable criticism.

**News coverage.** In local races with labor coverage, the union-label status of campaign materials occasionally appears in reporting. This is rare but not zero.

## The Practical Requirements for Union-Label Campaign Printing

### Confirm Union Affiliation Before Ordering

Ask any print shop you're considering: "Are you union-affiliated? What local? Can you provide documentation?"

A union shop will provide the union local name and number immediately, often offer a letter from the local confirming affiliation, and apply the correct bug format to your piece.

A shop that hedges, cannot name a specific local, or says "our workers have the option to join a union" is not union-affiliated in the way endorsement committees mean.

### Include the Union Bug in Your Design Specifications

Tell your designer or the print shop at the start of the project: "This needs to carry the union bug." Do not assume it will be added automatically. The specific bug format (which local, standard language) comes from the print shop — they know which local applies to their shop and what the bug looks like.

The bug typically appears:
- In the lower right or left corner of the front face
- Adjacent to the paid-for-by statement
- In a non-competing location that doesn't obscure the design

### Placement Near Legal Disclosures

New York campaign materials must include paid-for-by attribution. Federal campaign materials (congressional races) must include specific FEC disclaimer language. The conventional placement groups these together in the imprint area:

```
Paid for by [Campaign Committee Name]
[Union Bug]  [Printer Name, City]
```

This grouping makes the union affiliation immediately visible to anyone who examines the imprint area, which is exactly where endorsement committee members and informed union voters look.

## Campaign Mail Timing in the Hudson Valley

Hudson Valley political races — state assembly, state senate, county legislature, town board — follow a primary calendar (June) and general election calendar (November). Campaign mail has specific timing requirements:

**Primary campaigns:** Mail planning should begin in late March/early April for a June primary. Marketing Mail takes 3–10 days for delivery in the Hudson Valley; the mailing must drop no later than 10 days before the required in-home date.

**General election campaigns:** The critical window is October 1–25 for standard campaign mail. Mail dropping after October 20 runs risk of arriving after Election Day.

For campaigns with hard delivery deadlines (a piece must be in mailboxes before a specific event or debate), use First-Class mail rather than Marketing Mail — the 2–5 day delivery window is significantly more predictable.
