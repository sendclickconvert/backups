The difference between a file that goes to press smoothly and one that causes a delay — or worse, a reprint — comes down to a handful of specifications that most designers learn from making mistakes. This guide is the shortcut.

Whether you're a business owner working in Canva, a designer in InDesign, or a marketing coordinator submitting files to us at [Cornerstone Services](/services/printing) in New Paltz for the first time, here are the exact settings your print file needs.

## The Four Non-Negotiable Specs

### 1. Color Mode: CMYK, Not RGB

Your computer screen displays color in RGB — a combination of red, green, and blue light. Printers use CMYK — cyan, magenta, yellow, and black inks. The two systems produce different color ranges.

The problem: **vivid screen colors often don't exist in CMYK**. A saturated blue on a screen might print as a noticeably different shade in CMYK. If you build your design in RGB and submit it, the printer's system will convert it to CMYK automatically — but that conversion may shift colors in ways you didn't expect and won't catch until you see the printed piece.

The fix: Work in CMYK from the start. In Adobe InDesign or Illustrator, set your document color mode to CMYK when you create the file. In Photoshop, open images and convert to CMYK (Image > Mode > CMYK Color) before placing them. In Canva, the "Print" export option converts to CMYK, but not all design elements translate perfectly.

### 2. Resolution: 300 DPI at Print Size

DPI stands for dots per inch — the density of pixel information in an image. Screen images are typically 72 DPI. Print requires 300 DPI. At 72 DPI, a printed image looks blurry and pixelated.

The rule: **every photo or image placed in your print file must be at least 300 DPI at the size it will print**. If a photo is 300 DPI at 4 inches wide and you enlarge it to 8 inches wide in your design software, it becomes 150 DPI — not enough for print.

Check resolution in Photoshop: Image menu > Image Size. The resolution field shows DPI at the current print dimensions. If it's under 300, either source a higher-resolution version of the image or reduce the print size.

### 3. Bleed: 0.125 Inch on All Sides

Bleed is the extension of your background color or design beyond the intended trim edge. Commercial printers cut printed sheets to finished size using an industrial paper cutter. The cutter does not land in exactly the same position on every cut — there is a small tolerance of 1/16 inch or less.

If your background color or photo ends exactly at the trim edge and the cutter moves 1/16 inch inward, a thin white gap appears at the edge of the piece. Bleed eliminates this by extending the design 1/8 inch beyond the trim — if the cutter moves in any direction within its tolerance, the design still covers the full face.

**How to set it up:** Build your document at the finished size (e.g., 6 x 9 inches for a 6x9 postcard) plus 0.125 inch on each side (6.25 x 9.25 inches). Extend any backgrounds, photos, or design elements to the outer edge of this larger document. The printer's press PDF will include bleed marks showing where the trim will occur.

Designs with a white background that does not extend to the edge (a border design, for example) do not require bleed — there is no color to bleed.

### 4. Safe Zone: 0.125–0.25 Inch Inside the Trim

The inverse of bleed: just as you extend backgrounds beyond the trim, you keep critical content away from the trim edge. The 0.125 inch (1/8 inch) safe zone is the minimum — content placed closer to the trim edge risks being cropped in production.

For anything critical — your phone number, logo, CTA, a headline — stay at least 0.25 inch (1/4 inch) inside the trim edge. This gives the cutter's tolerance a margin on both sides.

## Font Handling: Outlined or Embedded

Fonts are software. If a PDF references a font that is not installed on the printer's system and is not embedded in the PDF, the printer's system substitutes a default font — usually something generic like Helvetica — replacing your typography.

**Solution 1: Outline your fonts.** In Illustrator, select all text and go to Type > Create Outlines. This converts the text from live, editable characters into vector shapes — no longer dependent on the font software being installed. The shapes cannot be edited as text afterward, so save an outlined version separately from your working file.

**Solution 2: Export as PDF/X-1a or PDF/X-4.** These PDF standards embed all fonts in the file automatically. InDesign's built-in PDF export presets handle this. For Illustrator and Photoshop, using the "Save As > Adobe PDF" option with the PDF/X-1a preset embeds fonts and flattens transparency.

## File Formats We Accept

| Format | Notes |
|--------|-------|
| PDF (preferred) | PDF/X-1a or PDF/X-4 for best compatibility |
| InDesign package | Include all linked files and fonts |
| Illustrator AI/EPS | Fonts must be outlined |
| Photoshop PSD | CMYK, 300 DPI, layers flattened or organized |
| Canva "Print" PDF | Use the PDF Print export option (not PDF Screen) |

**What we do not accept:** Word documents, PowerPoint files, or web image exports (JPG at 72 DPI). These require rebuilding the design at print resolution before production.
