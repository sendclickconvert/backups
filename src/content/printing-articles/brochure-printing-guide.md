A brochure does something a postcard cannot: it gives a buyer time to sit with your offer. The prospect who picks up a brochure at a trade show, tucks it in a bag, pulls it out that evening, and reads through all six panels is significantly further along the buying process than the postcard recipient who made a 3-second decision.

The brochure is a different tool with a different job. Here is how to choose the right format for the work you need it to do.

## Choosing the Right Fold

The fold determines how the reader experiences the content — which panel they see first, how the story unfolds, and how much information fits in each section.

### Tri-Fold (Letter Fold)

The most common brochure format. An 8.5x11 sheet folded into three equal panels, creating six faces. The right panel of the outside becomes the cover; the center panel of the outside becomes the back; the left panel tucks inside.

**Best for:** Service descriptions, company overviews, take-home information pieces, trade show handouts. The six panels give enough space to present three or four services, include photos, and add contact information without the piece feeling cramped.

**Print cost:** Generally the lowest of the fold options because the format is standard and most presses handle it automatically.

### Bi-Fold (Half-Fold)

An 8.5x11 sheet folded in half, creating four panels. More open and spacious than a tri-fold — each panel is larger, so photos and headlines have more room.

**Best for:** Premium service presentations, real estate property sheets, menus, event programs. The larger panels give a more open, upscale feel compared to a tri-fold. Also works well for 11x17 sheets folded to 8.5x11 — a larger, more impressive piece.

**Print cost:** Similar to tri-fold for the same sheet size. The larger format (11x17 folded to 8.5x11) costs more due to the larger press sheet.

### Z-Fold (Accordion Fold)

Six panels created by alternating fold directions, like an accordion. All panels are visible by unfolding one section at a time.

**Best for:** Step-by-step processes, timeline presentations, map-and-directions pieces. The sequential reveal creates a natural reading flow. More complex to set up — designer must understand which panels are "outward" and which are "inward" in the folded position.

**Print cost:** Same as tri-fold for the same sheet size, but z-folds require careful setup to prevent the panels from folding the wrong way.

### Gate Fold and Double Parallel Fold

More complex formats used for premium presentations. A gate fold has two outer panels that open like doors to reveal a large inner spread. Used for high-end product launches, luxury real estate, and corporate presentations.

**Print cost:** Significantly higher than standard folds — additional press steps and hand-folding may be required. We quote gate folds individually.

## Paper Stock for Brochures

Unlike postcards (which use cover stock for rigidity), brochures are typically printed on **text-weight** paper to fold cleanly:

**100 lb. gloss text:** The most common choice. Lighter than cover stock, folds smoothly without cracking, holds color well. What we print on for the majority of promotional brochures.

**100 lb. matte text:** Same weight as gloss text with a non-reflective surface. Popular for healthcare, professional services, nonprofits, and any context where a more sophisticated or clinical presentation is preferred.

**80 lb. gloss text:** Lighter — will work but feels less substantial. Appropriate for high-quantity handout pieces where cost is a priority, not appropriate for a premium sales presentation.

**Cover stock (80 lb. or 100 lb.):** Stiffer than text stock, used when you want the brochure to feel more like a card than a folded sheet. **Must be scored before folding** to prevent cracking along the fold line. We score all cover stock brochures as a standard step.

## Brochures as Direct Mail Self-Mailers

A brochure designed as a self-mailer removes the envelope cost and insertion step — the brochure itself carries the address panel and mails flat or folded.

**USPS requirements for self-mailing brochures:**
- All open edges must be tabbed (sealed with a wafer seal tab) or sealed — open edges cause the piece to catch in postal equipment
- Minimum one tab on the open edge for pieces under 10" in one dimension; two tabs for larger pieces
- Must meet USPS flat dimensions for the relevant mail class
- The address panel must be designed to USPS addressing standards with room for indicia, barcode, and return address

**Tab placement:** The standard position is one tab at the center of the open edge for tri-folds. We apply tabs as part of the mailing preparation process.

Alternatively, brochures can be inserted into standard #10 envelopes (for letter-size pieces folded appropriately) or large format envelopes for unfolded or lightly folded pieces.

## When a Brochure Is the Right Choice

Use a brochure when the buyer needs more information before committing. Service businesses with multiple offerings, complex pricing, or a process that requires explanation benefit from the brochure format. The prospect who needs to understand how your service works, what they get, and why you're different before calling is the brochure reader.

Use a postcard when you need the reader to act immediately on a specific offer. The 3-second decision that drives direct mail response rates is a postcard job, not a brochure job.

The strongest direct mail packages often combine both: an EDDM or targeted postcard with a strong offer drives calls; a brochure inserted in the response kit or handed over at the first appointment reinforces the brand and service detail.

## Design Best Practices for Brochures

The most common brochure design mistakes we see at Cornerstone — and how to avoid them:

**Panel layout errors on tri-folds.** A tri-fold brochure does not have three equal panels on each side. The panel that folds inward (the right panel on the inside) must be approximately 1/16 inch narrower than the other two panels so the piece folds flat without buckling. Designers who set up three equal panels at 3.667 inches each (on an 11-inch sheet) produce a piece that does not fold cleanly. The inside panel should be approximately 3.625 inches; the remaining two panels should each be approximately 3.6875 inches.

**Too much text, too little hierarchy.** A brochure has six panels (tri-fold) or four panels (bi-fold), but each panel is only 3.5 to 4 inches wide. Cramming full paragraphs of body text onto each panel makes the piece unreadable. The effective brochure uses bullet points, short paragraphs of 2–3 sentences maximum, and clear headlines on each panel. Each panel should convey one idea or one service category.

**Ignoring the fold in the design.** Photos, headlines, or design elements that span across a fold look excellent on a flat proof and terrible when folded — the image splits awkwardly, text breaks mid-word, or colors appear misaligned at the fold line. Design each panel as an independent unit. If you want a full-bleed image across multiple panels, discuss the alignment with your printer before production.

**Missing the address panel for self-mailers.** If the brochure will be mailed without an envelope, one panel must be designed as the address panel — with room for the mailing address, return address, postage indicia, and USPS barcode clear zone. This panel cannot carry your marketing message. Plan for it from the start; retrofitting an address panel into a finished brochure design wastes a panel and requires re-proofing.

## Paper Stock Selection by Industry

Based on the brochures we produce most frequently at Cornerstone, here are the stock and coating combinations that work best by industry:

**Home services (HVAC, plumbing, landscaping):** 100 lb. gloss text with UV coating. The glossy finish makes before-and-after photos pop and communicates a professional, established business. Tri-fold is the standard format — enough space for 3–4 service categories with photos.

**Healthcare and dental:** 100 lb. matte text. The subdued, non-reflective surface reads as clinical and professional. Patients associate matte-finish materials with medical offices and trust the content more than high-gloss promotional pieces.

**Real estate:** 100 lb. gloss text or 100 lb. gloss cover (scored for folding). Real estate brochures are photo-heavy — property images need the best possible color reproduction, and gloss stock delivers that. Many agents use bi-fold 11x17 (folds to 8.5x11) for individual property sheets.

**Nonprofits:** 80–100 lb. matte text. The understated finish communicates responsible stewardship of donor resources. Nonprofits that send high-gloss, expensive-looking brochures sometimes face donor perception issues — "why are they spending my donation on fancy printing?"

**Restaurants and hospitality:** 100 lb. gloss cover (scored), UV coated. Menus and promotional pieces need durability — a restaurant brochure that sits in a take-one rack for weeks needs stock and coating that resist handling wear.
